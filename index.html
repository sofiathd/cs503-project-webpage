Improving Temporal Understanding in Long-Form Activity Videos via Structure-Guided VLM Reasoning

Rim El Qabli,  Gauthier Nielly,  Lina Sadgal,  Sofia Taouhid
EPFL - CS-503 Visual Intelligence: Machines and Minds
Report Code TSU Dataset

Abstract

Video Large Language Models (Video LLMs) increasingly serve as unified architectures for video understanding, yet recent studies show they fail at temporal reasoning even on tasks specifically designed to measure it. Critically, this failure stems from temporal localization, not semantic understanding: VLMs recognize actions but cannot determine when they occur.

We propose a hybrid pipeline using temporal action detection (TAD) proposals as structural baseline to guide VLM reasoning on long untrimmed activity videos. ActionFormer within OpenTAD generates candidate segments, passed to a Video LLM (Qwen3-VL) for semantic labeling and composite activity inference.

We evaluate on Toyota Smarthome Untrimmed (TSU) dataset, where all prior work is TAD-only and no VLM evaluation exists, making ours the first VLM baseline on TSU. Our results reveal that VLMs correctly identify activity threads but hallucinate temporal structure, motivating the hybrid approach. We make the performance better than the pure VLM baseline, confirming that temporal scaffolding can improve VLM reasoning on long-form videos. Our structured analysis framework produces informative results regardless of outcome, concretely directing future work toward architectural improvements or better temporal proposals.

I. Introduction

Many real-world video applications share a common structure: a stationary camera records a long, continuous stream in which multiple actions compose into higher-level activities. Surveillance systems, industrial monitors, and smart-home cameras all require recovering full event timelines, not classifying single short clips.

Video LLMs are increasingly adopted as unified architectures for such tasks. However, TVBench demonstrated that most Video LLMs perform near random on temporal ordering and counting tasks, with shuffled input frames barely degrading performance — revealing reliance on static visual cues rather than genuine temporal reasoning. TemporalBench corroborated this: GPT-4o achieves only 38.5% accuracy versus 67.9% for humans, with performance declining further on longer videos.

Recent analyses identify a clear bottleneck: temporal localization, not semantic understanding. Wang et al. show that models like Qwen2-VL delegate temporal modeling entirely to the language backbone via spatial-only attention, which proves insufficient for fine-grained temporal dynamics. The MERIT framework further shows that correct visual cue identification is insufficient without temporal-causal inference.

Research Question:  Can explicit temporal structure from action detection improve VLM reasoning on long-form activity videos, and which aspects of timeline understanding benefit most from this combination?

Pure VLMs fail at temporal localization; pure TAD lacks semantic flexibility. No prior study has tested whether combining them resolves each other's weaknesses on long-form daily activity videos with dense hierarchical annotations. Our key contributions:

  • The first VLM evaluation on TSU, establishing a baseline where none exists.
  • A direct test of whether external temporal scaffolding at inference time can substitute for architectural changes to VLMs.
  • A structured analysis framework producing informative results regardless of outcome — each failure mode redirects future work concretely.

II. Related Work

Temporal Reasoning Failures in Video LLMs

TVBench demonstrated near-chance performance on temporal ordering and counting tasks, with shuffled frames barely affecting accuracy. TemporalBench confirmed this on fine-grained probes: GPT-4o reaches only 38.5% vs. 67.9% for humans. Wang et al. traced failures to spatial-only attention in vision encoders; MERIT showed that correct visual cue identification is insufficient without temporal-causal inference.

Segment-Then-Reason Approaches

T* iteratively selects key frames before reasoning about them. MR. Video applies a MapReduce paradigm across video segments, achieving state-of-the-art on long-video benchmarks. Structure-guided prompting improved temporal QA accuracy by up to 17.7% in autonomous driving scenarios. Our work tests whether this principle transfers to long-form daily activity videos with dense hierarchical annotations.

Temporal Action Detection on TSU

Prior TAD-only work on TSU includes PDAN (~32.7% frame-mAP) and the current state-of-the-art MS-Temba (2025), which reports CLIP features outperforming I3D on this benchmark. Crucially, no VLM has been evaluated on TSU, making our work the first to establish such a baseline.

Complementary Directions

Other work improves VLM temporal reasoning at the architecture or training level. Our approach is orthogonal: we provide external temporal structure at inference time without modifying model weights, a lightweight alternative applicable to any frozen VLM.

III. Method

Pipeline Overview

Our system operates in three stages:

  1. ActionFormer produces ranked candidate segments after NMS and confidence thresholding,
  2. For each proposal the VLM is queried with a structured prompt grounding it to a specific time interval and label set,
  3. Segment labels are merged chronologically with per-class NMS to preserve TSU's co-occurring activities.

Figure 1. Hybrid pipeline overview. ActionFormer (blue) generates temporal proposals; a VLM labels each proposed segment (green); outputs merge into a final timeline. The orange dashed branch is the pure-VLM baseline.

Dataset: Toyota Smarthome Untrimmed (TSU)

TSU provides 536 long untrimmed daily-activity videos (avgerage duration of 21 min) from fixed cameras in a real apartment, with dense annotations for 51 activity classes at 25 fps. We use the Cross-Subject (CS) protocol (subject-disjoint with no train/test overlap). We produce a derived ActivityNet-style annotation JSON with timestamps in seconds for compatibility with OpenTAD.

Baseline 1: ActionFormer (TAD)

We train ActionFormer within OpenTAD on the TSU CS split. Feature extraction uses CLIP ViT-B/32 at stride 16. We sample one frame every 0.64 s and pass each frame through CLIP's image encoder to get a 512-dimensional vector. Since TSU is not among OpenTAD's nine natively supported datasets, we wrote a TSU-specific config reusing OpenTAD's ThumosPaddingDataset class, the same approach used by Multi-THUMOS.

Baseline 2: Pure Video LLM

We evaluate Qwen3-VL-8B on TSU videos at N=32 uniformly sampled frames (2× NVIDIA V100, 64 GB VRAM). Models are queried with structured prompts requesting JSON timelines with labels constrained to the 51-class TSU ontology.

Hybrid Pipeline Design Decisions

TAD output → segment extraction → VLM labeling → timeline assembly

Our hybrid pipeline corresponds to a merge of our two baselines. Given a video, it is passed through ActionFormer to generate temporal proposals, which are filtered by NMS and confidence thresholding. Each proposal is then cropped into a segment and passed to the VLM with a structured prompt grounding it to that specific time interval and label set. Finally, outputs are merged chronologically with per-class NMS. Key design choices include per-class NMS to preserve TSU's co-occurring activities (multiple actions can happen simultaneously in domestic settings), ablation over confidence thresholds, and structured JSON prompting with fuzzy label matching to handle VLM outputs.

IV. Experiments

Evaluation Protocol — LCS Recall

Because all three systems (TAD, VLM, Hybrid) ultimately produce an ordered sequence of action labels per video, we evaluate them with a unified, model-agnostic metric: Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) Recall. For each video, ground-truth events from the TSU annotationsare are sorted by start_frame, predicted segments are sorted by start time and consecutive duplicates are collapsed. The score is the length of the LCS between the two sequences divided by the Ground Truth (GT) length, in percent:

$\text{LCS Recall} = \dfrac{|\text{LCS}(\text{GT}, \text{Pred})|}{|\text{GT}|} \times 100$

All labels are normalized before comparison. The metric rewards recall and correct ordering, is insensitive to extra (hallucinated) predictions, and applies identically to TAD output, VLM output, and the hybrid pipeline, making the three directly comparable.
