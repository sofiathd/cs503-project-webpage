Abstract

Vision Language Models (VLMs) increasingly serve as unified architectures for video understanding, yet recent studies show they fail at temporal reasoning even on tasks specifically designed to measure it. Critically, this failure stems from temporal localization, not semantic understanding: VLMs recognize actions but cannot determine when they occur.

We propose a hybrid pipeline using temporal action detection (TAD) proposals as structural baseline to guide VLM reasoning on long untrimmed activity videos. ActionFormer within OpenTAD generates candidate segments, passed to a Vision Language Model (Qwen3-VL) for semantic labeling and composite activity inference.

We evaluate on the Toyota Smarthome Untrimmed (TSU) dataset, where all prior work is TAD-only and no VLM evaluation exists, making ours the first VLM baseline on TSU. Our results reveal that VLMs correctly identify activity threads but hallucinate temporal structure, motivating the hybrid approach. Rather than a clear win, the hybrid pipeline redistributes performance: it trades the TAD baseline's high recall for higher precision, ending up roughly equivalent in F1 — the VLM filters redundant detections but does not recover more ground-truth actions. Our structured analysis localises why: failure is concentrated in rare, fine-grained action classes rather than driven by video length or complexity. The contribution is therefore a first baseline and a diagnostic framework that produces informative results regardless of outcome, concretely directing future work toward rare-class detection and temporal-boundary refinement.

Introduction

Many real-world video applications share a common structure: a stationary camera records a long, continuous stream in which multiple actions compose into higher-level activities. Surveillance systems, industrial monitors, and smart-home cameras all require recovering full event timelines, not classifying single short clips.

VLMs are increasingly adopted as unified architectures for such tasks, yet a growing body of work shows they fail at temporal reasoning, with benchmarks reporting near-chance performance on temporal ordering even when shuffling the input frames barely changes the result. Recent analyses pinpoint the bottleneck as temporal localization, not semantic understanding: VLMs recognize actions but cannot determine when they occur. (We review this evidence in detail in Related Work.)

Research Question:  Can explicit temporal structure from action detection improve VLM reasoning on long-form activity videos, and which aspects of timeline understanding benefit most from this combination?

Pure VLMs fail at temporal localization; pure TAD lacks semantic flexibility. No prior study has tested whether combining them resolves each other's weaknesses on long-form daily activity videos with dense hierarchical annotations. Our key contributions:

  • The first VLM evaluation on TSU, establishing a baseline where none exists.
  • A direct test of whether external temporal scaffolding at inference time can substitute for architectural changes to VLMs.
  • A structured analysis framework producing informative results regardless of outcome — each failure mode redirects future work concretely.
02

Related Work

Temporal Reasoning Failures in VLMs

TVBench demonstrated near-chance performance on temporal ordering and counting tasks, with shuffled frames barely affecting accuracy. TemporalBench confirmed this on fine-grained probes: GPT-4o reaches only 38.5% vs. 67.9% for humans. Wang et al. traced failures to spatial-only attention in vision encoders; MERIT showed that correct visual cue identification is insufficient without temporal-causal inference.

Segment-Then-Reason Approaches

T* iteratively selects key frames before reasoning about them. MR. Video applies a MapReduce paradigm across video segments, achieving state-of-the-art on long-video benchmarks. Structure-guided prompting improved temporal QA accuracy by up to 17.7% in autonomous driving scenarios. Our work tests whether this principle transfers to long-form daily activity videos with dense hierarchical annotations.

Temporal Action Detection on TSU

Prior TAD-only work on TSU includes PDAN (~32.7% frame-mAP) and the current state-of-the-art MS-Temba (2025), which reports CLIP features outperforming I3D on this benchmark. Crucially, no VLM has been evaluated on TSU — the gap this work sets out to fill.

Complementary Directions

Other work improves VLM temporal reasoning at the architecture or training level. Our approach is orthogonal: we provide external temporal structure at inference time without modifying model weights, a lightweight alternative applicable to any frozen VLM.

Method

We study the temporal-localisation gap from two angles. We first establish two independent baselines chosen to represent the opposite ends of the design space: a dedicated temporal action detector, which localises precisely but is restricted to a fixed label set, and a pure Vision Language Model, which is semantically flexible but weak at temporal grounding. We deliberately pick one model from each paradigm so that the hybrid can be measured against both — only then can we tell whether fusing them resolves each other's weaknesses rather than simply inheriting the stronger one. We then build a hybrid pipeline that fuses the two.

Dataset: Toyota Smarthome Untrimmed (TSU)

TSU provides 536 long untrimmed daily-activity videos (average duration of 21 min) from fixed cameras in a real apartment, with dense annotations for 51 activity classes at 25 fps. We use the Cross-Subject (CS) protocol (subject-disjoint with no train/test overlap). We produce a derived ActivityNet-style annotation JSON with timestamps in seconds for compatibility with OpenTAD.

Baseline 1: ActionFormer (TAD)

We pick ActionFormer as our TAD baseline because it is a strong, widely-used single-stage detector that is natively supported in OpenTAD, giving a reproducible reference point for the localisation side. We train it on the TSU CS split. Feature extraction uses CLIP ViT-B/32 at stride 16. We sample one frame every 0.64 s and pass each frame through CLIP's image encoder to get a 512-dimensional vector. Since TSU is not among OpenTAD's nine natively supported datasets, we wrote a TSU-specific config reusing OpenTAD's ThumosPaddingDataset class, the same approach used by Multi-THUMOS.

Baseline 2: Pure Vision Language Model

We pick Qwen3-VL-8B as our VLM baseline because it is a strong, open-weights video LLM that fits our hardware (2× NVIDIA V100, 64 GB VRAM), letting us probe the semantic side without proprietary-API constraints. We evaluate it on TSU videos at N=32 uniformly sampled frames, querying it with structured prompts that request JSON timelines with labels constrained to the 51-class TSU ontology.

Hybrid Pipeline

The hybrid pipeline combines the temporal precision of a dedicated action detector with the semantic flexibility of a Vision-Language Model. Rather than asking the VLM to reason over an entire 21-minute video, a setting where temporal grounding is known to fail, we use ActionFormer's proposals as a basis, restricting each VLM query to a short and well-localised clip. This design directly addresses the bottleneck identified in prior work: VLMs recognise actions but struggle with when they occur.

Figure 1. Hybrid pipeline. A raw video flows top-to-bottom through detection (TAD), three filtering steps (deduplication, confidence filter, per-class NMS), and VLM refinement, yielding a final JSON timeline. Dashed branches show segments that are dropped or suppressed along the way.

Stage 1: Temporal Proposal Generation

Using the CLIP features described above, ActionFormer produces a ranked list of candidate segments, each characterised by a start time, end time, predicted action label, and confidence score. The raw output is noisy by design, the detector intentionally over-proposes to maximise recall, so two filtering steps are applied before any VLM inference:

  • Confidence thresholding (τ = 0.15): segments below this score are discarded. This removes low-quality proposals while retaining borderline detections that the VLM may still be able to resolve.
  • Per-class Non-Maximum Suppression (IoU > 0.3): among overlapping proposals of the same class, only the highest-scoring survives. Crucially, NMS is applied per class rather than globally, preserving TSU's co-occurring activities. In domestic settings, a person can simultaneously Watch_TV and Eat_Snack, so cross-class suppression would incorrectly remove valid detections.

Stage 2: VLM Labeling

For each surviving proposal, the pipeline extracts 8 uniformly sampled frames from the corresponding video interval and constructs a structured prompt. The prompt grounds the VLM to the specific time window and provides the full 51-class TSU ontology, instructing it to return a single JSON object with a label and a confidence score. The TAD prediction is included as a hint and the VLM is asked to confirm or override it based on visual evidence. This framing keeps the VLM's role to be a semantic verifier, not a temporal reasoner.

Responses are parsed with a fuzzy label resolver that normalises punctuation and casing differences and applies whole-token boundary matching to map VLM outputs back to canonical TSU class names. When resolution fails, the original TAD label is preserved as a fallback, ensuring no segment is silently dropped due to an unparseable response.

Stage 3: Timeline Assembly

Labeled segments are sorted chronologically and a second round of per-class NMS (IoU > 0.3) is applied to the final output, removing any duplicates introduced by the VLM relabeling step. The result is a clean, ordered timeline of (start, end, label) triples for each video, directly comparable to TSU ground truth (GT) annotations.

This subset contains 6,731 annotated actions spanning 17 distinct task scenarios, with an average video duration of 16.9 minutes.

Evaluation Setup

The Density Mismatch Problem

A fundamental challenge is that GT annotations and model predictions operate at different densities: human annotators log salient, active interactions while omitting the passive background states that the VLM and TAD systems readily label (we illustrate this concretely in the example below).

This creates a structural mismatch: a predicted sequence of 250 actions being compared against a GT of 80 does not mean the model is wrong 170 times, it often means the model is more thorough than the annotator. Any metric that penalises insertions will unfairly punish the denser system.

Why Not Exact Match or Edit Distance?

Exact match requires identical sequence length and content. Given the density mismatch described above, it would score near zero for every video regardless of labeling quality, it is simply the wrong tool for this comparison.

Edit distance (Levenshtein) charges a unit cost for every insertion, deletion, and substitution. Each extra Watch_TV prediction that the annotator simply skipped would incur a penalty, conflating being more thorough with being wrong.

Why LCS Recall?

The Longest Common Subsequence captures the largest set of GT actions that appear in the predicted sequence in the correct chronological order, ignoring any extra predictions in between. Formally:

$\text{LCS Recall} = \dfrac{|\text{LCS}(\text{GT}, \text{Pred})|}{|\text{GT}|} \times 100$

This has three properties that make it appropriate here:

  • Insertion-insensitive. Extra VLM predictions between matched GT actions are simply skipped, not penalised. The question becomes: “did the model recover what the annotator logged?”
  • Order-sensitive. Unlike set-based metrics (e.g. label overlap), LCS requires the matched actions to appear in the same temporal order in both sequences. A model that hallucinates the correct labels in the wrong order is correctly penalised.
  • Metric-agnostic across systems. Because the metric operates on ordered label sequences rather than timestamps, it applies identically to TAD output, VLM output, and the hybrid pipeline, making all three directly comparable without requiring timestamp alignment or IoU matching between predicted and GT segments.

Reporting Alongside Precision and F1

LCS Recall alone is not sufficient as a sole metric, since a system predicting thousands of random labels would score perfectly. We therefore also report LCS Precision, defined symmetrically as $|\text{LCS}| \,/\, |\text{Pred}|$, and the harmonic F1. Together, these three numbers capture both coverage (recall) and prediction quality (precision), giving a complete picture of each system’s trade-offs.

Even without reading the numbers, the structural asymmetry between the three systems is immediately apparent.

The Density Mismatch in Practice

The GT contains 60 sparsely distributed annotations logged by a human annotator. TAD produces 205 predictions across the same duration, and the Hybrid pipeline keeps 172. Human annotators on TSU record salient, active interactions such as Get_Up, Drink_From_Cup, or Take_Something_Off_Table, while omitting passive background states that persist between them. Both TAD and the VLM, by contrast, actively identify and label these intermediate states (Watch_TV, Sit_Down, Use_Laptop) because they are visually present throughout the video. This asymmetry is structural, not a failure of either system, and it is the primary reason why evaluation metrics must be chosen carefully.

TAD’s Localisation Problem

Looking at the TAD row, several large monolithic blocks are visible, most notably an extended segment spanning roughly minutes 1 through 8. This corresponds to a single label held across a long continuous duration, a pattern that has no equivalent in the GT. This reveals a core limitation of the TAD baseline: even when the predicted label is semantically correct, the temporal boundaries are too imprecise to constitute a meaningful localisation. At IoU 0.5, a segment that covers the right action but spans three times the annotated duration scores zero, regardless of label correctness. TAD’s high LCS Recall is partly an artifact of this over-extension: a long predicted segment overlapping several GT actions allows the LCS algorithm to match many GT actions against a single prediction, inflating recall without reflecting genuine temporal understanding.

What the Hybrid Pipeline Changes

The Hybrid row is visibly more fragmented and colour-diverse than TAD, indicating that the VLM actively relabels and subdivides the large TAD segments into shorter, more varied predictions. This is reflected in the improved precision of the Hybrid system, fewer predictions are wasted. However, the Hybrid row remains substantially denser than the ground truth, particularly in the second half of the video (minutes 10–20), where the ground truth records a single long passive state while both systems continue predicting activity. This confirms the central finding of our qualitative analysis: the VLM, influenced by the TAD segmentation, identifies more activity threads than the human annotator logged, which is not strictly wrong, but cannot be rewarded by a metric anchored to sparse human annotations.

Recall vs. Precision Trade-off

The results reveal that TAD and the Hybrid pipeline do not represent a strict performance ordering, they make fundamentally different trade-offs. TAD achieves the highest LCS Recall but the lowest Precision, meaning it recovers most GT actions through sheer prediction volume rather than targeted localisation. The Hybrid pipeline sacrifices recall (49%) in exchange for higher precision (30.7%), as the VLM filtering removes many of the redundant TAD predictions. Crucially, the LCS F1 scores are nearly identical, confirming that the harmonic mean correctly normalises for the density difference and reveals the two systems to be roughly equivalent in overall sequence recovery quality. The Hybrid pipeline does not degrade performance relative to TAD, it redistributes it.

mAP Results

The segment-level mAP scores are low across both systems (Hybrid: 5.9% at IoU 0.5), reflecting the temporal boundary imprecision illustrated in the qualitative example. These numbers are not directly comparable to prior TSU work such as PDAN (32.7% frame-mAP), which uses a different and more lenient evaluation protocol. They do, however, confirm that neither system achieves reliable temporal localisation under strict overlap criteria, and motivate future work on boundary refinement.

Per-Class Analysis

Figure 3. LCS Recall per action class (classes with ≥5 occurrences, 86 videos), sorted descending. Computed via LCS traceback to identify exactly which ground-truth actions were matched.

The figure above shows LCS Recall broken down by action class, computed via LCS traceback to identify precisely which GT actions were matched. Only classes with at least 5 occurrences across the test set are included for statistical reliability; the remaining classes scored zero recall entirely.

High-Recall Classes

The top-performing classes (Get_Up, Sit_Down, Use_Cupboard, Cook_Cut, Drink_From_Cup, Use_Fridge) all share a common property: they are visually unambiguous and spatially distinctive. These actions involve clear postural changes, specific appliances, or object interactions that produce consistent visual signatures across subjects and camera angles. TAD’s temporal features, trained on CLIP representations, reliably detect the onset and offset of these actions, and the VLM confirms them correctly when queried. Recall above 80% for these classes suggests the pipeline has genuinely learned to recognise them.

The Middle Tier

Classes scoring between 40% and 65% (Watch_TV, Cook, Enter, Use_Laptop, Read) are common but visually ambiguous. These are often background or transitional states that co-occur with other activities and lack a precise temporal boundary. The pipeline detects them but not always at the right moment or in the right order relative to other actions, which LCS penalises. Notably, Walk and Put_Something_On_Table fall into this tier despite being among the three most frequent classes in the dataset, frequency alone does not guarantee recall, confirming that visual distinctiveness matters more than training signal volume for this pipeline.

Low-Recall Classes

The bottom third of the chart reveals a sharp drop-off. Classes such as Take_Something_Off_Table (25%), Drink_From_Bottle (18%), Write (7%), and Breakfast_Spread_Jam_Or_Butter (4%) score near zero despite having sufficient occurrences. Two failure modes are present. First, several of these are fine-grained sub-actions of a broader category: Drink_From_Bottle versus Drink_From_Cup, or Make_Coffee_Pour_Water versus Make_Coffee. The pipeline predicts the parent class rather than the specific sub-action, scoring zero under exact label matching. Second, brief interactions like Write or Pour_From_Bottle occupy short temporal windows that TAD’s sliding-window detector systematically misses or merges into adjacent segments.

Zero-Recall Classes

The classes at the very bottom (Pour_From_Kettle, Make_Tea_Insert_Tea_Bag, Make_Tea_Boil_Water, Make_Coffee_Get_Water, Use_Glasses, Stir_Coffee_Tea, Pour_From_Can, Drink_From_Glass) score exactly 0% despite meeting the minimum occurrence threshold. These are exclusively rare, fine-grained sub-actions. Their zero recall is a direct consequence of training distribution bias: ActionFormer was trained on a dataset where these classes appear so infrequently that the model never learned to reliably detect them. The VLM similarly has little basis to override a TAD prediction toward these obscure sub-classes. This is the most concrete finding of the per-class analysis: the pipeline’s failure is concentrated in a predictable subset of rare, fine-grained classes, not distributed uniformly across all actions.

Robustness to Video Complexity

Figure 4. LCS Recall vs. video complexity (total ground-truth actions per video) across 86 videos. The regression line (red) shows a mild negative trend, but the wide confidence band and high scatter indicate complexity is a weak predictor of recall.

The figure above plots LCS Recall against video complexity, measured as the total number of GT actions per video. The regression line shows a mild negative trend (recall decreases slightly as videos become more complex) but the wide confidence band and high scatter indicate that complexity is a weak predictor of performance at best.

What the Trend Tells Us

The negative slope is statistically present but practically modest: a video with 200 GT actions scores roughly 5–8 percentage points lower than a video with 50 GT actions on average. This suggests the pipeline degrades gracefully with complexity rather than collapsing, which is a positive robustness finding. Long videos do not systematically break the pipeline.

What the Scatter Tells Us

The variance at low complexity (videos under 50 GT actions range from 7% to 96% recall) is far larger than the variance explained by the trend line. This means that for short videos, other factors dominate, most likely which specific action classes appear in the video. A short video dominated by high-recall classes like Get_Up and Use_Fridge will score near 90%, while a short video containing mostly fine-grained sub-actions like Write and Pour_From_Bottle will score near 10%, regardless of length.

Synthesis

Taken together, the per-class analysis and the robustness plot suggest that the primary axis of failure is action class identity rather than video duration. A video’s recall score is more predictable from knowing which classes it contains than from knowing how long it is. The Pearson correlation between GT action count and recall is r = −0.28 (p < 0.05), confirming a statistically significant but weak negative relationship. Future work should focus on improving rare-class detection rather than scaling to shorter videos.
